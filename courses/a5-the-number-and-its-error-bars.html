The number and its error bars

Every metric you are handed is a single number pretending to be the truth. "Conversion is 3.2%." But that 3.2% came from a sample, and a different week's sample would have said 3.1% or 3.4%. The honest version of any estimate carries an error bar - a range that says "here is our best guess, and here is how sure we are." This session teaches you to read that error bar, to demand it when a chart hides it, and to stop treating a point estimate as if it were carved in stone. No math you have to do, no code.

Part 0

"Conversion is 3.2%" - to how many decimal places do you believe that?

Lumen Skincare's dashboard shows a checkout conversion rate of 3.2%. It looks exact. It is not. That number was measured on a sample of sessions, and if you had watched a different slice of traffic you would have seen a slightly different figure. The real answer is not "3.2%" - it is "somewhere around 3.2%, and here is the range we are confident about." The width of that range is the honest part. A narrow range means we know it well; a wide range means we are guessing. Learn to read the range and you stop over-reacting to numbers that were never as precise as they looked.

Part 1 · the honest number

Point estimate vs interval estimate 8 min live

A single number - "3.2% conversion" - is a point estimate. It is our single best guess. The honest version carries a margin of error: "3.2% +/- 0.5pp", which is the same as saying the true rate is somewhere in [2.7%, 3.7%]. That range is the interval estimate. The one rule that governs everything: bigger samples make the interval tighter. More data, more certainty, narrower error bar.

LiveThe point is the guess, the interval is the honesty3 min▶

Two ways to report the same finding, one of them dangerously incomplete:

  • Point estimate - the single number. "3.2% conversion", "AOV is $74". Easy to say, easy to put on a slide, and silent about how sure we are.
  • Interval estimate - the number plus its margin. "3.2% +/- 0.5pp" or "[2.7%, 3.7%]". It admits the guess has wiggle room and tells you exactly how much.
  • Margin of error - the half-width of the interval. It shrinks as the sample grows, so a bigger, longer measurement buys you a tighter answer.
Real world

Two Lumen channels both "convert at 3.2%." A director wants to declare them equally good. But channel A was measured on a tiny slice of traffic and its interval is [2.0%, 4.4%]; channel B ran all quarter and its interval is [3.0%, 3.4%]. Same headline, completely different confidence. Channel A might truly be a 2% dud or a 4.4% star - we genuinely do not know yet. Reading the interval, not the point, stopped the room from betting on a coin flip.

LiveWhy bigger samples buy tighter intervals3 min▶

The link between sample size and confidence is not a nice-to-have - it is the whole reason we collect more data. The margin of error shrinks as the sample grows, though with diminishing returns: to halve the error bar you roughly need four times the data.

  • Small n, wide bar - a week of traffic gives a shaky estimate. The interval is broad because the sample could easily have landed elsewhere.
  • Large n, narrow bar - a quarter of traffic pins the number down. The interval is tight because we have seen enough to rule out the extremes.
  • The exec instinct: when a surprising number lands, the first question is not "is it true?" but "how many observations is it based on?" A big swing on a small sample is usually noise.
Your leverage is the question, not the calculation You never compute a margin of error in a meeting. You ask "what is the range on that, and how many data points is it based on?" That single habit turns a room full of confident point estimates into an honest conversation about what is actually known.
Part 2 · the one big idea

What a 95% confidence interval really means 7 min live

Here is the phrase you will see everywhere and the meaning almost everyone gets wrong. A 95% confidence interval does not mean "95% chance the true value sits in this particular range." It means the procedure - the method of drawing a sample and building an interval around it - captures the true value 95% of the time across many repeated samples. Draw 20 samples, build 20 intervals, and about 19 of them will contain the truth. This is the ★ idea of the session, and unlearning the three wrong readings is where the real leverage is.

LiveThe three wrong readings to unlearn4 min▶

The correct reading is: the procedure that built this interval captures the true value 95% of the time over many repeated samples. The confidence is in the method. Now the three seductive misreadings that a careful leader refuses:

  • Wrong #1 - "there is a 95% chance the true value is inside THIS interval." Tempting, but no. The true value is a fixed number; it is either in this particular interval or it is not. The 95% describes how often the procedure succeeds, not this one bar.
  • Wrong #2 - "95% of the data lies inside this interval." No. A confidence interval is about the location of an estimate (like a mean or a rate), not about where individual data points fall. The raw orders can be far more spread out than the interval.
  • Wrong #3 - "95% of future results will land in this interval." No. It says nothing about individual future outcomes. Next week's rate could easily sit outside it. That is a different kind of range (a prediction interval), and it is much wider.
Real world

On a Lumen dashboard, two channels show error bars that overlap heavily - one centered at 3.2%, one at 3.4%. A PM wants to crown the 3.4% channel the winner. But overlapping intervals are the visual signal that the "difference" is probably just noise; the data cannot yet tell them apart. The right move is not to declare a winner - it is to gather more data or, better, run a proper test (Session 6). Reading the overlap saved a budget shift built on a mirage.

Self-studyConfidence level vs width, and why error bars belong on every exec chart4 min read▶

There is a knob on every confidence interval: how confident do you want to be? And it trades directly against how useful the answer is.

  • Higher confidence, wider interval. A 99% interval is wider than a 95% one, which is wider than a 90% one - because to be more sure of catching the truth, you have to cast a bigger net. There is no free lunch: certainty costs precision.
  • 95% is the convention, not a law of nature. It is a reasonable balance of "sure enough" against "narrow enough to be useful." Pick 99% when a miss is expensive; a wider bar is the price.
  • Why error bars belong on every exec chart: a bar chart of point estimates invites the room to over-read tiny differences. Add the error bars and the honest story appears - which gaps are real and which are noise become visible at a glance.
A number without a range is half a fact When a chart shows bare bars with no error bars, it is not being clean - it is hiding its uncertainty. Ask for the intervals. If two bars' intervals overlap, treat "they are different" as unproven until a test says otherwise.
Before Session 6

This week ◐ 25 min total

  • Find one point estimate in a recent report - a rate, a conversion, an average. Ask its owner: what is the margin of error, and how many observations is it based on? Write down whether the answer changes how much you trust it.
  • Practice the correct reading. Find a "95% confidence interval" anywhere (a report, a news article, a survey) and say out loud what it actually means - the procedure captures the truth 95% of the time - and catch yourself if you slip into one of the three wrong readings.
  • Hunt overlapping error bars. Find a chart comparing two groups. If it has error bars, check whether they overlap. If it does not, note that the chart is hiding its uncertainty.
  • Optional: pick one decision you would make on a 95% interval and ask whether you would want 99% instead - and whether you would accept the wider range that comes with it.
Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · A 95% confidence interval for Lumen's conversion is [2.7%, 3.7%]. Which reading is correct?

Confidence is a property of the procedure, not of any single interval. The true value is fixed; it is the interval that varies from sample to sample. A and B are two of the classic wrong readings.

2 · Two Lumen channels both estimate 3.2% conversion. Channel A's interval is [2.0%, 4.4%]; channel B's is [3.0%, 3.4%]. What is the honest takeaway?

Same point estimate, very different confidence. A's wide interval means small sample and real uncertainty; B's narrow interval means we have pinned it down. Reading the width, not the point, is the whole skill.

3 · On a dashboard, two bars have error bars that overlap heavily. What should a careful leader conclude?

Overlapping intervals are the visual signal that a "difference" may be noise. Treat it as unproven until more data or a proper test (Session 6) settles it.
