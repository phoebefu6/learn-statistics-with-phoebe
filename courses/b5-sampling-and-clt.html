Sampling and the Central Limit Theorem

You never measure the whole population - you sample it, and every sample gives a slightly different answer. This session makes that wobble concrete: draw thousands of samples from Lumen's skewed order values, watch the distribution of their means turn into a bell curve, and measure how fast it tightens. This one idea - the CLT - is the engine behind every confidence interval and A/B test in the rest of the course.

Part 0

One sample is a guess - the shape of many samples is a law

Take 100 Lumen orders and average them: you get a number near $74, but not exactly. Take another 100, another number. Do that thousands of times and something remarkable happens - those averages pile up into a clean, symmetric bell, even though the raw order values are stubbornly right-skewed. That bell has a known width, and knowing its width is what lets you say "the true mean is $74, give or take." Today you build that bell with your own hands.

Part 1 · the vocabulary in code

Sampling distributions, standard error, and the CLT 6 min live

Three ideas stack on top of each other. Get them in order and the rest of the course is downhill.

LiveThe three ideas, in order3 min▶
  • Sampling distribution of a statistic. Any statistic you compute (a mean, a median, a proportion) is itself random - draw a new sample and it changes. The distribution of that statistic across many samples is the sampling distribution. It is the thing statistics actually reasons about.
  • Standard error. The standard deviation of that sampling distribution has a name: the standard error. For a mean it is SE = sigma / sqrt(n). More data (bigger n) means a narrower sampling distribution, but only as fast as the square root - quadruple n to halve the SE.
  • The Central Limit Theorem. As n grows, the sampling distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution, regardless of the shape of the population. Skewed, bimodal, lumpy - it does not matter. The mean goes normal. That universality is why the normal curve is everywhere in statistics.
Read the theorem carefully The CLT does not say your data becomes normal. Lumen order values are right-skewed and always will be. It says the average of a sample of them behaves normally once n is large enough. Confusing the two is the single most common mistake in applied statistics.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd

df = pd.read_parquet("lumen_orders.parquet")   # built in b1

print("N      :", ov.size)              # 50000

The money demo. Draw 2,000 sample means for n = 2, 10, 50 and watch the bell arrive.

clt_demo.py 
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
REPS = 2000

def sample_means(data, n, reps, rng):
    # reps samples of size n, return the mean of each
    idx = rng.integers(0, data.size, size=(reps, n))
    return data[idx].mean(axis=1)

for n in (2, 10, 50):
    means = sample_means(ov, n, REPS, rng)
    print(f"n={n:>2}  mean-of-means={means.mean():6.2f}  "
          f"std-of-means={means.std():5.2f}  "
          f"skew={pd.Series(means).skew():+.2f}")
# n= 2  mean-of-means= 74.0x  std-of-means=26.9x  skew=+0.6x  (still lumpy)
# n=10  mean-of-means= 74.0x  std-of-means=12.0x  skew=+0.2x  (rounding out)
# n=50  mean-of-means= 74.0x  std-of-means= 5.3x  skew=+0.0x  (normal)

The mean of the sample means stays glued to the population mean (~$74). What changes is the spread - it shrinks - and the skew - it melts toward zero. That melting is the CLT happening in front of you.

Verify the standard error. The spread you just measured should match sigma / sqrt(n).

verify_se.py 
sigma = ov.std()             # population SD ~ 38

for n in (2, 10, 50):
    means = sample_means(ov, n, REPS, rng)
    observed = means.std()               # spread of the sampling distribution
    formula  = sigma / np.sqrt(n)         # the SE formula
    print(f"n={n:>2}  observed={observed:5.2f}  "
          f"formula={formula:5.2f}  ratio={observed/formula:4.2f}")
# ratios land near 1.00 - the formula and the simulation agree.
# n=50: 38 / sqrt(50) ~= 5.37, and your histogram width ~= 5.3.
Why sqrt(n) and not n Halving the standard error costs 4x the data, not 2x. This square-root wall is why big experiments are expensive - and exactly the arithmetic that sets the sample sizes you will compute in b7 and in the experimentation course.

Bootstrap - a standard error with no formula. Resample the data with replacement, recompute the mean each time, and read the spread straight off the resamples.

bootstrap.py 
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
B = 2000
n = ov.size

boot_means = np.empty(B)
for b in range(B):
    resample = rng.choice(ov, size=n, replace=True)   # same size, WITH replacement
    boot_means[b] = resample.mean()

se_boot = boot_means.std()
ci_lo, ci_hi = np.percentile(boot_means, [2.5, 97.5])   # 95% percentile CI

print("bootstrap SE  :", round(se_boot, 3))          # ~ 0.17
print("formula   SE  :", round(ov.std()/np.sqrt(n), 3))  # ~ 0.17  they agree
print("95% CI for mean:", [round(ci_lo, 2), round(ci_hi, 2)])  # ~ [73.7, 74.3]
Real world

The bootstrap is the practitioner's escape hatch. When you need a standard error for something with no clean formula - a median, a 90th percentile, a conversion-weighted average order value - you resample and measure the spread directly. No distributional assumption, no lookup table. It is the same engine as the CLT, run by brute force, and it is why every confidence interval and A/B readout in the rest of this course rests on the ideas you built today.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Lumen order values are strongly right-skewed. What does the Central Limit Theorem promise as sample size n grows?

The CLT is about the sampling distribution of the mean, not the raw data. Order values stay skewed forever; the average of a sample of them goes normal as n grows. That is the single most misread sentence in statistics.

2 · Your sample-mean histogram at n=10 has spread ~12. You move to n=40. Roughly what spread do you expect?

SE = sigma / sqrt(n). Going from n=10 to n=40 multiplies n by 4, which multiplies sqrt(n) by 2, so the standard error is halved: 12 becomes about 6. Quadruple the data to halve the wobble.

3 · What is the defining feature of a bootstrap resample when estimating a standard error?

Bootstrapping resamples the observed data itself at the original size, with replacement, so some points repeat and some drop out. The spread of the statistic across those resamples is the standard error - no formula and no distributional assumption required.
