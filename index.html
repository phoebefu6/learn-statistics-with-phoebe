SEC-02 THE THREE SHAPES

Almost every number a business tracks falls into one of three shapes. Name the shape and you can read the number properly.

normalbell

  • heights, errors
  • averages of anything
  • typical: the centre
  • swings: symmetric

binomialtries

  • successes out of
  • a fixed set of tries
  • typical: rate x tries
  • swings: widest at 50%

poissonrate

  • events per stretch
  • of time
  • typical: the rate
  • swings: grow with count
Why the shape comes first

The shape tells you what is typical, what is surprising, and how wide the swings should be. Read a count as if it were a bell and every unusual week looks like a crisis.

SEC-03 THE HONEST NUMBER

A single number is a best guess. The honest version carries its margin of error, and the margin is the part people drop.
Point estimate against interval
point estimate 3.2% small sample [2.0% 4.4%] large sample [3.0% 3.4%]

Same best guess, very different confidence. Bigger samples make the interval tighter: more data, more certainty, narrower error bar.

3.2% +/- 0.5pp is the same claim as [2.7%, 3.7%]. If two error bars overlap, "different" is probably just noise.
The one rule that governs it

Bigger samples make the interval tighter. Same best guess, more certainty, narrower bar. Every confidence interval on the sheet is that one rule applied.

SEC-06 THE FALLACY GAUNTLET

p-hackingTest twenty noise features and one clears the bar by luck. Ask: how many things were tested before this one?
Correlation is not causationThe line fits and still explains nothing. Ask: what else moved at the same time?
Simpson's paradoxGrouping the same rows can reverse the verdict outright. Ask: does this hold inside every segment?
Base-rate neglectA test that is 99 percent accurate is still mostly wrong on a rare event. Ask: how common is the thing being detected?
The over-trusted averageThe mean is the most over-trusted number in business, and one outlier moves it. Ask: what does the median say?
Two bars that overlapTwo error bars touching is not two different results. Ask: do the intervals overlap?
A correlation of zero can hide a perfect curve. Pearson measures straight lines only; Spearman catches anything monotonic.
Why this is the capstone, not the footnote

Every fallacy here is an honest calculation. Nobody lied, nobody mistyped a formula. The number is right and the reading is wrong, which is exactly why the fallacy survives a review that only checks the arithmetic.
